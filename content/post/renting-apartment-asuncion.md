Renting an apartment, Asunción-style, starts with picking a barrio before you start scrolling listings — the city's neighborhoods vary more in character and commute than in raw price, and getting that part right matters more than shaving a few dollars off the monthly rent. This guide walks through how the market actually works, what landlords expect from a tenant, and the questions worth asking before you sign anything.

## How apartment hunting works here

Most rentals in Asunción are found through local real estate agencies (inmobiliarias) rather than the kind of national listing portals you might be used to, plus a fair number of word-of-mouth and Facebook-group listings that never make it onto a website at all. Agencies typically charge a commission — sometimes to the landlord, sometimes split, sometimes to the tenant alone — so it's worth asking upfront who pays what before you view anything. Photos on listings are often outdated or flattering, so plan to see a place in person before committing; a short trip that includes viewings is far more efficient than negotiating a lease sight unseen.

Furnished apartments rent at a premium and are the more practical option for a stay under a year, since furnishing an unfurnished place from scratch (and reselling everything on the way out) eats into any savings. Longer-term residents more often go unfurnished, buying furniture gradually and sometimes reselling it to the next expat leaving town.

## Apartment, Asunción: which neighborhood fits you

Villa Morra and Carmelitas/Manorá are the most popular with expats and remote professionals — walkable, close to Shopping del Sol and a dense cluster of cafés and restaurants, and generally well served by fiber internet. Recoleta is quieter and leafier, popular with families, and still an easy drive to the center. Sajonia sits further out but offers noticeably lower rents for similar apartment quality, at the cost of a longer commute. ## Contracts, deposits, and what landlords expect

Standard leases run one or two years, typically with rent fixed in US dollars even though day-to-day payments are made in guaraníes at the exchange rate on the day of payment — confirm which currency and which exchange-rate method the contract uses before signing, since this detail varies by landlord. A security deposit of one to two months' rent is standard, held against damage and unpaid utilities. Foreigners without a Paraguayan guarantor (garante) sometimes need to pay a larger deposit upfront or provide a few months' rent in advance instead, since the local system leans heavily on a guarantor vouching for the tenant. Bring your passport, proof of income or a bank reference, and be ready to negotiate this point directly — it's one of the more foreigner-specific frictions in an otherwise straightforward process.

Utilities (electricity, water, and internet) are usually billed separately from rent and set up in the tenant's name for longer leases, or included in the price for furnished short-term rentals aimed at expats. Ask explicitly which utilities are included before you calculate your real monthly cost, since "all-inclusive" listings vary in what that actually covers.

## Practical info for apartment hunters

- **Best way to search:** local inmobiliarias and expat Facebook groups, more than international listing sites
- **Typical lease length:** one to two years for unfurnished; more flexible (monthly or a few months) for furnished, expat-oriented rentals
- **Deposit:** commonly one to two months' rent, sometimes more without a local guarantor — confirm the exact terms in writing
- **Currency:** rent is frequently quoted in US dollars but paid in guaraníes — clarify the exchange-rate method in the contract
- **Documents to bring:** passport, proof of income, and (if you have one) a local guarantor or bank reference
- **Best time to look:** outside the December–February heat and holiday season, when more landlords and agents are actively available to show apartments

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a Paraguayan guarantor to rent an apartment?
Many landlords prefer one, but it's not universal — plenty accommodate foreigners with a larger deposit or a few months of rent paid upfront instead. Ask directly and compare a few listings, since policies vary by landlord rather than being set city-wide.

### Is it better to rent furnished or unfurnished?
For stays under a year, furnished is almost always more practical once you account for the cost and hassle of buying and later reselling furniture. For a multi-year stay, unfurnished tends to work out cheaper over time.

### What's a fair deposit to pay?
One to two months' rent is standard practice, though this can be higher for foreign tenants without a local guarantor. Get the deposit terms, including what counts as damage, written into the contract rather than agreed verbally.

### Which neighborhood should I choose if I don't know Asunción yet?
Villa Morra or Carmelitas/Manorá are the easiest first choice for most newcomers — central, walkable, and well stocked with the cafés and shops that make the first few months easier.
