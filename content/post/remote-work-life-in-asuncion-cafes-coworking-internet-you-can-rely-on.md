Asunción is not (yet) on the standard digital-nomad circuit the way Medellín or Lisbon are, and that is part of its appeal. Remote work life in Asunción means short commutes, cheap living costs, and a slower pace, but it also means you have to be more deliberate about where you plug in than you would in a city with a coworking space on every block. This guide covers the cafés that tolerate a laptop for hours, the coworking spaces worth a membership, and how to get internet at home that won't drop out mid-call.

## Cafés with wifi you can actually work from

The safest bet for a working coffee is a café inside or next to one of the big shopping centers — Shopping del Sol in Villa Morra and Paseo La Galería near Aviadores del Chaco both have multiple cafés with air conditioning, real chairs, and wifi that's shared across dozens of tenants, so it rarely goes down completely even when one connection hiccups. Villa Morra and the Carmelitas/Manorá strip are the other reliable pocket: a walkable cluster of specialty coffee shops that have gotten used to laptop crowds in the last few years, with outlets along the walls and staff who won't rush you out after one cortado.

Two things to plan around. First, weekend and lunchtime rushes push the good tables — mid-morning on a weekday is your best window. Second, air conditioning in Asunción's heat (October through March especially) is not optional if you're taking video calls, so prioritize places that clearly run AC over ones that just have big windows open. Bring a phone hotspot as backup on a second SIM; cafés share one connection across the whole room, and that connection occasionally saturates during peak hours.

## Coworking spaces

Dedicated coworking spaces exist in Asunción but the scene is smaller and less standardized than in bigger regional capitals — expect a handful of options concentrated in Villa Morra and the microcentro rather than a space in every neighborhood. What you're paying for over a café is a dedicated desk, faster and more consistent bandwidth (often a business-grade line rather than a shared residential one), meeting rooms you can book for client calls, and a community of other people working odd hours. If you're in Asunción for more than a couple of months, it's worth walking into a few spaces near where you're staying, asking to see the actual internet speed on their router rather than taking their word for it, and asking about day-pass options before committing to a monthly plan — check current membership prices before you go, since these change more often than the spaces' addresses do.

## Setting up reliable home internet

If you're renting an apartment for a longer stint, fixed fiber internet from one of the main providers — Tigo, Personal, or Claro — is widely available in central Asunción neighborhoods like Villa Morra, Recoleta, Carmelitas, and Sajonia, and installation is usually quick once you have a lease and an ID. Fiber has made mobile-hotspot backups less necessary than they used to be, but it's still worth keeping a second SIM with a healthy data plan as insurance: outages happen, usually after heavy summer storms, and Paraguay's mobile networks are generally solid even when fixed lines go down. If you're apartment hunting specifically with connectivity in mind, ask the landlord or agent directly whether fiber is already installed in the building — some older buildings in otherwise central areas still only have copper lines, which cap out well below what a video-call-heavy workday needs.

## Practical info for remote workers

- **Best neighborhoods to base yourself:** Villa Morra, Carmelitas/Manorá, and Recoleta for the density of cafés and short commutes to coworking spaces
- **Time zone:** Paraguay runs on its own time zone (UTC-4, or UTC-3 during the country's daylight-saving period), which lines up conveniently with US Eastern for calls
- **Power:** 220V, plugs are the European-style round two-pin (type C) — bring a universal adapter if you're arriving from North America
- **SIM cards:** buy a local SIM from Tigo, Personal, or Claro at the airport or any of their stores — bring your passport, and check current data plans before you commit to one
- **Backup connectivity:** keep a mobile hotspot or second SIM as insurance against café or home wifi dropping during a call
- **Best time to arrive and settle:** outside the December–February heat peak if you can help it, so apartment-hunting and getting set up is more comfortable

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the internet in Asunción fast enough for video calls?
Fiber connections in central neighborhoods generally handle video calls without issue, and most cafés and coworking spaces cater to laptop users specifically. The exception is older buildings on copper lines rather than fiber, so it's worth confirming what's installed before you sign a lease if calls are central to your work.

### Do I need a Paraguayan bank account to work remotely from here?
Not for short stays — many remote workers get by on foreign cards and cash for months. For longer stints, a local account simplifies rent payments and avoids repeated foreign-transaction fees, but it typically requires residency paperwork, so it's not a same-week task.

### Are there dedicated digital-nomad meetups in Asunción?
The community is smaller and less organized than in nomad hubs elsewhere in Latin America, so it's more about finding your own regulars at a handful of cafés and coworking spaces than joining a large formal scene. That's changing gradually as more remote workers pass through.

### What's the best area to live in if I work from home full time?
Villa Morra and Carmelitas/Manorá are the most convenient for the combination of cafés, coworking options, and short trips to shops or the airport — see our [neighborhood guide](/best-neighborhoods-to-live-in-asuncion-where-should-you-settle-down-in-2025/) for a fuller comparison across the city.

### How much does it cost to live and work remotely in Asunción compared to home?
Significantly less than most North American or Western European cities for rent, food, and transport, though imported electronics and some packaged goods cost more. Our [cost of living guide](/cost-of-living-paraguay/) breaks down realistic monthly budgets.
