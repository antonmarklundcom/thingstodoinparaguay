Paraguay national parks rarely make it onto a first-time South America itinerary, and that is
exactly why they're worth the detour. The country sits at the meeting point of three very
different ecosystems — the Atlantic Forest in the east, the vast wetlands of the Pantanal in the
far north, and the dry Chaco scrubland to the west — and its protected areas give visitors a real
shot at seeing that range in a country most travelers only pass through on the way to Iguazú. Trail
infrastructure is modest compared to Brazil or Argentina's national parks, which is part of the
appeal: you're more likely to have the forest to yourself than to share it with a tour bus.

## Cerro Corá: the most visited of Paraguay national parks

[Cerro Corá National Park](/cerro-cora-park/) (also see its
[background on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_Cor%C3%A1_National_Park)), near the
Brazilian border in Amambay department, is
probably the most visited of Paraguay's national parks, and for two reasons. First, it's where
Paraguay's president Francisco Solano López was killed in 1870, at the end of the devastating War
of the Triple Alliance — a monument marks the spot, and the park carries real weight in national
memory. Second, it's simply striking terrain: isolated granite hills (cerros) rise out of grassland
and forest, with short but rewarding hikes to viewpoints over the surrounding plains. It's
manageable as a half-day or full-day visit if you're already exploring the northeast of the
country.

## Ybycuí: Atlantic Forest close to Asunción

Parque Nacional Ybycuí — reachable
in roughly two to three hours from Asunción — protects one of the largest remaining fragments of
Paraguay's Atlantic Forest, the same ecosystem that once stretched across much of eastern Paraguay,
southern Brazil and northeastern Argentina before large-scale clearing for agriculture. Inside the
park, trails wind past waterfalls, including the popular Salto Guaraní, and through dense, humid
forest that shelters birdlife you won't see in the drier Chaco. The ruins of a 19th-century iron
foundry inside the park add a layer of industrial history to the natural setting. Ybycuí is the
easiest genuine rainforest experience for anyone based in the capital without a long drive.

## The Chaco and the Pantanal: harder to reach, harder to forget

West of Asunción, across the Paraguay River, the landscape changes completely. The Gran Chaco is a
huge, sparsely populated expanse of dry forest and scrubland shared with Argentina and Bolivia —
one of the largest continuous dry forest ecosystems left on the continent. Protected areas here,
along with the Mennonite colonies around [Filadelfia](/filadelfia-chaco/), see far fewer visitors
than the eastern parks simply because of distance and road conditions; a Chaco trip needs planning,
ideally a 4x4 and more travel time than a weekend allows.

To the far north, wetland reserves bordering the [Pantanal](/pantanal-paraguayo/) — the world's
largest tropical wetland, shared with Brazil and Bolivia — protect some of the country's richest
wildlife habitat, with caimans, capybaras and an exceptional range of birdlife. This is genuinely
remote travel, usually arranged through a specialist operator with boat or light aircraft access,
rather than a self-drive weekend trip.

## Practical information

- **Best time to visit:** the dry season, roughly May to September, gives easier trail access and
  fewer mosquitoes; the wetter months (October to March) bring lush green forest but muddier paths.
- **Getting there:** Cerro Corá and Ybycuí are reachable by rental car or organized tour from
  Asunción; the Chaco and Pantanal reserves are far more remote and generally need a guide or tour
  operator with local knowledge of road and water conditions.
- **What to bring:** sturdy footwear, insect repellent, a refillable water bottle, and a hat — shade
  is limited on the Chaco's open scrubland and on Cerro Corá's exposed hilltops.
- **Facilities:** basic at best — check current hours and any entry requirements before you go, since
  ranger stations are not always staffed and signage can be limited compared to parks in neighboring
  countries.
- **Guides:** hiring a local guide, especially in the Chaco or Pantanal, adds real value — they know
  the wildlife-viewing spots and the safest routes, and it supports the small communities that live
  alongside these parks.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many national parks does Paraguay have?

Paraguay has a network of national parks and other protected areas spread across its three major
ecosystems — Atlantic Forest, Chaco and Pantanal wetland — though not all are developed for tourism
or easy for independent travelers to reach. Cerro Corá and Ybycuí are the most visitor-ready.

### Which Paraguay national park is easiest to visit from Asunción?

Ybycuí, roughly two to three hours by road, is the most accessible option for anyone based in the
capital who wants a genuine rainforest and waterfall experience without a multi-day trip.

### Do I need a guide to visit the Chaco or Pantanal parks?

It's strongly recommended. Distances are long, roads can be rough or seasonal, and local guides
know both the wildlife and the practical logistics — fuel, water, safe river crossings — far better
than a visitor arriving independently.

### Is it safe to hike alone in Paraguay's national parks?

The eastern parks like Cerro Corá and Ybycuí have marked trails and see enough visitors that solo
hiking on the main paths is generally fine, though telling someone your route and expected return
time is sensible anywhere with limited signal. Remote areas like the Chaco call for more caution
and, ideally, a guide.

### What wildlife might I see?

Expect birdlife across all the parks, plus capybaras and caimans in wetland areas near the
Pantanal, and a chance — though never a guarantee — of spotting larger mammals like tapirs or foxes
in the less-visited Chaco reserves.
