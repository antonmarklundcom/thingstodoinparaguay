Paraguay sightseeing rewards patience more than any other country in the region. There's no
single postcard monument pulling in crowds — no Machu Picchu, no Iguazú-scale spectacle inside its
own borders — which means most visitors arrive with low expectations and leave surprised by how
much there actually is to see once you start looking. This guide sorts the country's best sights
into three tiers: what's walkable in Asunción, what's an easy day trip, and what's worth a longer
detour if your itinerary allows it.

## Paraguay sightseeing in Asunción: colonial core and riverside views

Start in the capital. The **Manzana de la Rivera**, a cluster of restored colonial-era houses near
the Government Palace, gives a compact sense of Asunción's layered history in about an hour. From
there, the **Palacio de los López** (the seat of government, viewable from outside) sits on a bluff
over the Paraguay River, and the nearby **Costanera de Asunción** is a long riverside promenade
good for an evening walk as the city cools down. The **Panteón Nacional de los Héroes**, a small
domed mausoleum modeled loosely on Les Invalides in Paris, holds the remains of several national
figures and is worth five minutes even if you're not a history buff. For a fuller list of stops
around the capital, see our guide to [what to do in Asunción](/what-to-do-in-asuncion/).

## Easy day trips from the capital

Once you've covered the city center, several worthwhile sights sit within a couple of hours' drive:

- **[Caacupé](/caacupe/)**, home to the Basílica de Caacupé, the most important pilgrimage site in
  the country and a striking piece of architecture even outside its December festival period.
- **[San Bernardino](/san-bernardino/)**, a lakeside town on Lake Ypacaraí popular with Asunceños
  for weekend escapes, with a laid-back café and restaurant scene.
- The **Jesuit ruins** further south — see below — if you're willing to make it a longer day or an
  overnight trip.
- **[Cerro Corá National Park](/cerro-cora-park/)** and the wider network of
  [Paraguay's national parks](/paraguay-national-parks/), if you'd rather trade plazas and ruins for
  hiking and open landscapes for a day.

Most of these day trips are doable independently by rental car, but Paraguayan intercity buses tend
to run on their own schedule, so build in flexibility if you're relying on public transport. Renting
a car for a day or two, or booking a driver, removes a lot of the guesswork and lets you set your
own pace rather than working around a bus timetable.

## Further afield: ruins, waterfalls and the Chaco

For visitors with more than a few days, three sights justify the extra travel time. The
[Jesuit missions](/jesuit-missions-paraguay/) near Encarnación, a UNESCO-recognized set of
[Jesuit Reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit_Reductions) ruins, are the closest thing
Paraguay has to a must-see historical monument — atmospheric stone ruins that see a fraction of the
visitors similar sites draw in neighboring countries. Further east, [Iguazú Falls](/iguazu-falls/)
is technically on the Argentine-Brazilian border but is easily combined with a Paraguay trip via
Ciudad del Este. And to the west, the Chaco region around
[Filadelfia](/filadelfia-chaco/) offers a completely different Paraguay: Mennonite colonies, dry
scrubland, and a slower, frontier pace that contrasts sharply with the humid east.

## Practical information

- **Best time to go:** April to September (the dry season) for the most comfortable sightseeing
  weather; December's Caacupé pilgrimage is a spectacle in its own right if you don't mind crowds.
- **Getting around:** Asunción's sights cluster within walking distance of each other downtown; day
  trips are easiest by rental car or organized tour, since regional bus schedules can be sparse.
- **What to bring:** comfortable walking shoes, sun protection, and small bills for local buses and
  informal vendors — cards aren't accepted everywhere outside the capital.
- **Pace yourself:** Paraguay's sights are spread out rather than clustered, so trying to see
  everything in two or three days usually means a lot of driving and not much time to actually look
  around. Pick a tier and go deep rather than wide.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the single best thing to see in Paraguay?

Most visitors rate the Jesuit mission ruins near Encarnación as the standout, both for their
historical weight and the fact that they're rarely crowded compared to similar UNESCO-listed sites
elsewhere in South America.

### How many days do I need for Paraguay sightseeing?

Three or four days covers Asunción and one or two nearby day trips comfortably. A week or more lets
you add the Jesuit missions and, if you're driving, a taste of the Chaco.

### Is Paraguay sightseeing safe for tourists?

The main sightseeing areas in Asunción and the towns mentioned here are generally fine for tourists
exercising normal city-travel caution. For a fuller picture, see our
[Paraguay travel advice](/paraguay-travel-advice/) guide and check your government's current travel
advisory before you go.

### Can I see Iguazú Falls from a Paraguay-based trip?

Yes — Ciudad del Este sits close to the Brazilian and Argentine border crossings, and day trips to
the falls are a common add-on for travelers already in eastern Paraguay. See our
[Ciudad del Este guide](/exploring-ciudad-del-este/) for the border-crossing logistics.

### Do I need a guide for the Jesuit ruins?

Not strictly, but a guide adds a lot of historical context that on-site signage doesn't always
cover, especially around the mission system's role in colonial-era Guaraní life.

Paraguay sightseeing works best when you treat the lack of a single headline attraction as a
feature, not a flaw — it's a country better explored slowly, one plaza or ruin at a time, than
rushed through on a checklist. If you'd rather have the logistics handled, [ask us to build a
custom day-trip itinerary](/day-trips-asuncion/) around whichever tier of sights fits your time.
