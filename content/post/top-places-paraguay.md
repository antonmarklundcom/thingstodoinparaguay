Paraguay rarely makes anyone's first list of South American destinations, which is exactly why it
rewards the people who do go. There are no crowds at the ruins, no queues at the falls, and no
sense that a place has been shaped around tourism rather than around the people who actually live
there. If you are trying to work out the top places Paraguay has to offer for a first visit, this
is a practical shortlist built around how the country actually connects geographically, not just a
list of pretty photos.

## Top places Paraguay offers: Asunción first

Almost every trip starts in Asunción, the capital, and it deserves more than a layover. The
historic centre still has its neoclassical government palace and a scattering of older buildings
along the waterfront, though much of the city is modern and low-rise by South American capital
standards. Costanera Asunción, the riverside promenade, is the best place for an early evening
walk, and the newer Villa Morra and Recoleta neighbourhoods have the city's better restaurants and
cafés. Two or three days is enough to see the centre, eat well, and use the capital as a base for
day trips before moving on. For a fuller sense of what to prioritise once you land, see our guide
to [what to do in Asunción](/what-to-do-in-asuncion/).

## The Jesuit missions around Encarnación

Southeast of the capital, near Encarnación, the ruins of Trinidad and Jesús del Tavarangüe are
among the best-preserved Jesuit reduction sites in South America, predating and in some ways
rivalling the more famous ruins across the border in Argentina and Brazil. Trinidad in particular
has an intact bell tower and detailed stone carving that gives a real sense of the scale these
mission towns reached in the 1600s and 1700s before the Jesuits were expelled. Encarnación itself
sits on the Paraná River facing Argentina and makes a comfortable base for a night or two, with its
own reworked costanera and beach club scene in summer. Read more in our
[Jesuit missions guide](/jesuit-missions-paraguay/), and see the
[Jesuit Reductions on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit_Reductions) for the broader
history.

## Ciudad del Este and Iguazú Falls

At the eastern tip of the country, Ciudad del Este is Paraguay's second city and a major duty-free
shopping hub for visitors crossing from Brazil and Argentina, but its real draw is proximity: it
sits close enough to the Iguazú Falls and the Itaipu Dam that both are practical day trips. Itaipu
itself is one of the largest hydroelectric dams in the world, jointly run with Brazil, and its
visitor programme is worth the detour even if dams aren't usually your thing. See our guides to
[Ciudad del Este](/exploring-ciudad-del-este/) and [Iguazú Falls](/iguazu-falls/) for details, and
[Itaipu Dam on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itaipu_Dam) for background on the project.

## The Chaco and the Mennonite colonies

West of Asunción, across the Río Paraguay, the landscape changes completely. The Gran Chaco is a
vast, sparsely populated dry forest that stretches into Argentina and Bolivia, home to some of the
best wildlife-spotting in the country and a genuine frontier feel. Filadelfia and the surrounding
Mennonite colonies, settled by German-speaking migrants in the 20th century, are one of the more
unexpected places anywhere in South America: working farms, cheese factories and a museum that
tells the settlement's story. It is a long drive from Asunción and not a rushed add-on, more a
destination in its own right for travellers with a few extra days. Our
[Filadelfia and the Chaco](/filadelfia-chaco/) guide has route notes, and the
[Gran Chaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Chaco) and
[Mennonites in Paraguay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mennonites_in_Paraguay) Wikipedia pages give
useful context before you go.

## Lake towns and the interior: San Bernardino, Caacupé and Cerro Corá

Closer to the capital, Lake Ypacaraí's shoreline towns are Asunción's traditional weekend escape.
San Bernardino, on the lake's northeastern edge, has old summer houses and a relaxed, slightly
faded resort-town atmosphere. Caacupé, a short drive further on, is Paraguay's most important site
of religious pilgrimage, built around the Basilica of the Virgin of Caacupé. Further north, near
the Brazilian border, Cerro Corá National Park protects the site where the Paraguayan War ended in
1870, alongside hiking trails through low granite hills. All three are manageable as day trips from
Asunción or can be strung together into a longer loop through the interior.

## Practical information

- **Best time to go:** April to October (Paraguay's dry season and cooler months) for comfortable
  travel; December to February is intensely hot and humid, especially in the Chaco.
- **Getting around:** long-distance buses connect all the main towns cheaply, but distances are
  real: Asunción to Ciudad del Este or to Filadelfia is a full day on the road, so plan accordingly
  or arrange a private driver for tighter itineraries.
- **What to bring:** light, breathable clothing, a hat and sunscreen, insect repellent for rural
  and forest areas, and a reusable water bottle, since Paraguay's heat is no joke most of the year.
- **Currency:** the guaraní; check current exchange rates and carry some cash, particularly for
  smaller towns where card acceptance is inconsistent.
- **Pace:** don't try to do the Chaco, the missions and Iguazú in one short trip. Pick one or two
  regions beyond Asunción and do them properly rather than rushing all of them.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many days do I need to see the top places in Paraguay?

A week gives you a comfortable loop covering Asunción, one lake or interior town, and either the
Jesuit missions or the Chaco. Ten days to two weeks lets you add Ciudad del Este and Iguazú without
rushing.

### Is Paraguay safe for tourists?

Most visitor experiences are calm and low-incident, with the usual city-travel precautions applying
in Asunción and Ciudad del Este. For current, official guidance, check the
[US State Department's Paraguay travel advisory](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/traveladvisories/traveladvisories/paraguay-travel-advisory.html)
before you travel, and see our own [is Paraguay safe](/is-paraguay-safe/) guide for more detail.

### Do I need Spanish to travel around Paraguay?

It helps a great deal outside Asunción's tourist-facing businesses. Guaraní is also widely spoken,
especially in rural areas, but Spanish will get you through almost any practical situation.

### Can I visit Iguazú Falls from Paraguay, or do I need to go through Argentina or Brazil?

Ciudad del Este is close enough that Iguazú works well as a day trip from the Paraguayan side, with
onward access to the Brazilian or Argentine sides of the falls depending on your route and visa
situation. Check current border and entry requirements before you cross.

### What's the single best first stop if I only have a few days?

Asunción plus one nearby excursion, either San Bernardino and Caacupé or the Jesuit missions near
Encarnación if you can stretch to an overnight. Both give a real sense of the country without a
long drive — see our [San Bernardino trip](/san-bernardino-trip/) if that's the one you pick.
