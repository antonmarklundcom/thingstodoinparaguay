Paraguayan cuisine gets overshadowed by its neighbors — Argentina's beef culture and Brazil's
regional variety both cast a long shadow — but it has a genuinely distinct identity, shaped less by
Spanish colonial cooking than by Guaraní ingredients: corn, cassava (mandioca), and the wild greens
and herbs that grow across the country's subtropical interior. If you come expecting a variation on
Argentine or Brazilian food, you'll be surprised by how much of the everyday table here runs on
starches other than wheat and on mate rather than coffee.

## Paraguayan cuisine's corn-and-cassava backbone: chipa

Start with **chipa**, the bread that appears everywhere from bus terminals to Easter tables. It's
made from cassava starch and cornmeal, bound with cheese and egg, and baked into small rings or
buns — dense, slightly chewy, and sold hot from clay ovens on street corners across Asunción. Chipa
has dozens of regional variations, and Paraguayans eat it constantly, not just as a festive food,
though the days around Holy Week (Chipa Guasu season) are when production goes into overdrive
nationwide.

Related, and easy to confuse for a first-timer, is **sopa paraguaya** — despite the name, it's not
a soup at all, but a dense cornbread made with cheese, onion and milk. The story goes that a cook
serving a 19th-century Paraguayan president let a corn soup thicken too much and served it as a
solid dish anyway; whether or not that's exactly how it happened, the dish stuck, and it's now a
fixture at any asado or Sunday lunch table.

## Asado and the meat course

As in the rest of the Southern Cone, **asado** — slow-grilled meat over wood coals — is central to
Paraguayan family and social life, usually on weekends. What sets a Paraguayan asado apart is what
surrounds the meat: sopa paraguaya, chipa guasu (a softer, more custard-like cousin of sopa
paraguaya, studded with whole corn kernels), and mandioca boiled and served plain alongside the
meat, functioning the way bread or potatoes might elsewhere. If you're invited to a family asado
during your trip, go — it's one of the most genuine ways to experience Paraguayan food culture, far
more than any restaurant menu.

## Terere: the national drink, not just a snack

No discussion of Paraguayan food culture is complete without
**[terere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terer%C3%A9)**, cold [yerba mate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yerba_mate)
drunk through a metal straw from a shared guampa, usually with ice water and sometimes medicinal
herbs (yuyos) mixed in. Where Argentina and southern Brazil drink mate hot, Paraguay's heat makes
the cold version the everyday norm, and you'll see groups sharing a guampa on porches, in parks and
at bus stops throughout the day — it's as much a social ritual as it is a drink. If you want to
understand terere culture hands-on rather than just watching it, a

## Practical information

- **Must-try dishes:** chipa, sopa paraguaya, chipa guasu, asado, and terere or hot mate (tereré in
  summer, mate cocido or hot yerba mate in cooler months).
- **Best time for chipa:** available year-round, but Holy Week sees the widest variety and freshest
  supply as families bake in bulk.
- **Where to eat:** food parks for variety and low commitment, traditional parrillas for a proper
  asado, and Mercado 4 in Asunción for an unfiltered look at how locals actually shop and eat.
- **Dietary notes:** vegetarian options exist (chipa, sopa paraguaya and most sides are meat-free)
  but the culture is meat-centric, so vegetarians should expect to ask specifically at asado-focused
  restaurants.
- **What to bring:** an appetite and, if you can find one, a willingness to try terere from a shared
  guampa — it's a social gesture as much as a drink.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most typical Paraguayan dish?

Chipa is the single most iconic food — sold everywhere, eaten by everyone, and instantly
recognizable as Paraguayan. For a full meal, an asado with sopa paraguaya and chipa guasu on the
side is the classic spread.

### Is sopa paraguaya actually a soup?

No, despite the name it's a dense, savory cornbread with cheese and onion. It's one of the more
famous quirks of Paraguayan cuisine and a good conversation starter with locals.

### What's the difference between terere and mate?

Both use yerba mate leaves and a metal straw (bombilla), but terere is served cold with ice water
and sometimes herbs, while mate is served hot. Paraguay's heat makes terere the default for most of
the year.

### Is Paraguayan food spicy?

Generally no — Paraguayan cuisine leans savory and starchy rather than spicy, in contrast to some
other Latin American food traditions. Chili isn't a staple ingredient here.

### Where can I try a range of Paraguayan dishes in one place?

different vendors in one visit, or a guided food tour if you'd like context along with the food.
