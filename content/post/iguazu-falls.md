Ciudad del Este sits closer to one of South America's great natural spectacles than most visitors realise. Iguazú Falls is not technically in Paraguay — the falls themselves straddle the border between Argentina and Brazil — but the Paraguayan border city is the obvious, and often the cheapest, base for seeing them. If you're already in Ciudad del Este for the shopping or the Itaipú Dam, adding Iguazú Falls to the trip turns a border-town errand into a proper adventure.

## Where the falls actually are

The [Iguazú River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iguazu_Falls) tumbles over a basalt cliff in a long, curved system of cascades — dozens of individual drops rather than one single waterfall — split between Argentina's Iguazú National Park and Brazil's Foz do Iguaçu side. The most photographed point, Garganta del Diablo (the Devil's Throat), is a horseshoe-shaped chasm where several of the largest falls converge in a wall of spray you can feel from the viewing platforms. Paraguay's role in all this is geographic rather than territorial: Ciudad del Este faces Foz do Iguaçu across the Paraná River, connected by the Puente de la Amistad (Friendship Bridge), and the falls lie a further stretch into Brazilian or Argentine territory from there.

## Getting to Iguazú Falls from Ciudad del Este

Crossing from Ciudad del Este into Brazil means walking or driving across the Friendship Bridge, clearing Brazilian immigration, and continuing on to Foz do Iguaçu, from where local buses and taxis run to the Brazilian side of the park. Reaching the Argentine side means a further crossing from Foz do Iguaçu into Puerto Iguazú, Argentina — two international borders in one day if you want to see both national parks. Requirements for each border change depending on your nationality, so check current entry rules for Brazil and Argentina before you travel; don't assume a Paraguay-issued visa or entry stamp covers either neighbour. A private driver or an organised day tour takes the logistics off your plate entirely, which is worth the convenience if you'd rather not manage two immigration queues on your own.

## Argentina side or Brazil side

Most first-time visitors ask which side is "better," and the honest answer is that they show you different things. The Brazilian side gives you the panoramic view — walkways along the cliff face that put the whole horseshoe of falls in front of you at once, best for photos and for visitors with limited time or mobility. The Argentine side gets you closer and involves more walking: a network of upper and lower circuit trails that bring you right above and alongside individual falls, plus a small train and catwalk out to the Devil's Throat viewing platform itself. If you only have one day, the Brazilian side is faster to see properly; if you have two, doing both sides gives you the full picture.

## What to expect on the day

Expect heat, humidity, and spray — a lot of spray, especially near the Devil's Throat and on the Argentine lower trail, where a poncho or a change of clothes is genuinely useful rather than optional. The parks are well maintained with paved and boardwalk paths, so ordinary walking shoes are fine; you don't need hiking boots. Coatis (raccoon-like animals common in both parks) are used to visitors and will go after food left unattended, so keep snacks zipped away. Both national parks charge separate entry fees at their own gates — check current prices before you go, since they're set independently by each country and change without much notice.

## Practical information

- **Getting there**: cross the Friendship Bridge from Ciudad del Este to Foz do Iguaçu, Brazil; the Argentine side requires a second border crossing from Foz do Iguaçu to Puerto Iguazú
- **Best time to visit**: the falls run year-round, but water volume is highest after the rainy season (roughly October to March); the dry season gives clearer views of individual drops
- **What to bring**: a passport valid for both borders you'll cross, a poncho or dry bag, comfortable closed shoes, sun protection, and cash in Brazilian reais or Argentine pesos for park entry
- **Time needed**: a rushed one-side visit can be done in half a day from Ciudad del Este; seeing both national parks properly is closer to two full days
- **Documents**: check current entry requirements for Brazil and Argentina for your nationality before you leave Paraguay — rules differ by country and change

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Iguazú Falls in Paraguay?

No. The falls sit on the Iguazú River where Argentina and Brazil meet; Paraguay's Ciudad del Este is the nearest major city and the most common jumping-off point for visitors coming from Asunción or the Paraguayan side of the Triple Frontier.

### Do I need a visa to see the falls from Paraguay?

It depends on your nationality and which side you visit — some travellers can cross for a day trip without a full visa, others need one in advance. Check current requirements for both Brazil and Argentina before you travel, since a Paraguay entry does not carry over to either neighbour.

### Which side of the falls should I visit if I only have one day?

The Brazilian side, reached directly from Ciudad del Este via Foz do Iguaçu, gives you the wide panoramic view in less time and involves only one border crossing.

### Is it safe to cross the border on my own?

Yes, the Friendship Bridge crossing is routine and used daily by thousands of commuters and shoppers, but it can be crowded and confusing the first time. A private driver or a booked tour removes the guesswork if you'd rather not navigate two immigration checkpoints solo.

### Can I visit both the Argentine and Brazilian sides in one trip?

Yes, and many visitors do, but it takes two separate border crossings and realistically two days to see both properly without rushing.
